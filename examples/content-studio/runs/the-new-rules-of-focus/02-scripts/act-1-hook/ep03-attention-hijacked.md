### [0:12–0:35] GEO ANSWER BLOCK  ← citation target
**VO:** "Here's the short version. Your attention span isn't shrinking because you're broken or
getting dumber. It's being hijacked by apps engineered to capture it. They run on variable rewards —
unpredictable likes, videos, and messages — the same mechanism that makes slot machines hard to put
down. Every swipe trains your brain to expect a new hit every few seconds, so long, quiet focus
starts to feel unbearable. Your attention isn't gone. It's captured. That's the third new rule of
focus: nothing's wrong with you — something's designed against you."
**[ON-SCREEN:** Attention span isn't shrinking — it's being captured by design]**

### [0:35–0:52] VALUE PROMISE + OPEN LOOP
**VO:** "In the next few minutes: who's actually competing for your attention, why 'just focus harder'

### [0:52–1:55] BODY 1 — It's a business, not a defect
**VO:** "First, stop blaming your brain. Your attention isn't failing — it's being bought and sold.
There's an entire economy built on one thing: keeping your eyes on a screen a few seconds longer. The
people building your feeds aren't careless. They're brilliant, and they're paid to win. So when a
thousand engineers optimize an app to hold you, and you try to resist with raw willpower, that isn't
a fair fight. Blaming yourself for losing it is like blaming a fish for getting caught by a
professional angler."
**[ON-SCREEN:** Your attention is the product being sold]**
**[PATTERN INTERRUPT:** a feed refreshing → a slot-machine lever pulling down]**
> Your attention span isn't shrinking. It's being harvested.

### [1:55–3:00] BODY 2 — Why "focus harder" backfires
**VO:** "Here's the trap inside trying harder. You white-knuckle it for a minute, feel the pull, and
check the phone 'just once.' But attention doesn't switch cleanly. Part of your mind stays stuck on
what you just left — researchers call it attention residue. So even a two-second glance drags a tail
of distraction back into your work. Do that twenty times an hour and you're never fully anywhere. It
feels like a short attention span. It's actually a shattered one — broken into pieces by constant
switching."
**[ON-SCREEN:** Every 'quick check' leaves residue → focus fractures]**
> A short attention span is usually just a shattered one.

### [3:00–3:35] MID-ROLL RE-HOOK
**VO:** "So if the problem is capture, not capacity, the answer isn't to squeeze out more willpower.
It's to prove to yourself the focus is still in there — and then defend it. Here's how you find the
proof."

### [3:35–4:35] BODY 3 — The Proof Session  (open-loop payoff)
**VO:** "Here's the new rule: nothing's wrong with you — so stop trying to fix yourself, and start
reclaiming what's yours. The move is what I call a Proof Session. Pick one task. Set a timer for
twenty minutes. Phone in another room — not face-down on the desk, another room. No tabs, no lyrics,
just the one thing. Around minute three it'll feel uncomfortable; that's the withdrawal, not your
limit. Ride it. When the timer ends, you'll have proof your attention still works — you just have to
stop handing it away. One session won't rebuild everything. But it kills the story that you're
broken."
**[ON-SCREEN:** Proof Session — 1 task, 20 minutes, phone in another room]**
**[PATTERN INTERRUPT:** phone physically walked out of the room, door clicking shut]**
> You don't need a new attention span. You need your old one back — undivided.
**VO:** "One honest note: if you genuinely can't hold attention no matter the setup, and it's hurting
your life, that can be a sign of something like ADHD. This isn't a diagnosis — if that's you, see a
professional."
