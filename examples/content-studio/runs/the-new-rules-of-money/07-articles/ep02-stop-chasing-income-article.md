# Should You Chase Income or Build Wealth?

*Companion article for The New Rules 101 · Episode 2 of "The New Rules of Money." Educational /

---

**Build wealth. Chasing income feels productive, but it's a treadmill — you can run faster forever
and never actually move. Income is money that shows up *because you show up*; the moment you stop
working, it stops. Wealth comes from ownership — money that keeps working after you clock out. A
bigger paycheck makes you comfortable. Only ownership makes you free.** That's the second new rule
of money: own more to get wealthy, not just earn more.

Here's why income alone never gets you there. When a raise arrives, the lifestyle arrives right
behind it — a nicer place, a newer car, upgraded everything — and the gap between what you make and
what you keep stays exactly zero. That's why high earners go broke: athletes, doctors, executives
on huge salaries, one bad month from panic. A high income is a fast-moving river. Impressive to
watch, but water that rushes past you isn't water you own.

The shift is simple. Stop asking "how do I earn more?" and start asking "how much of what I earn do
I get to *own*?" Wealth is the well you dig beside the river — and the first well you dig is the gap
between what you earn and what you spend.

---

## Why income isn't wealth
A big income and a big life tend to grow together. The raise lands, spending expands to match, and
you feel busier without feeling freer. Income impresses people; ownership frees you. If earning more
were the same as being wealthy, no high earner would ever go broke — yet it happens constantly,
because a rising paycheck with no ownership underneath it is just a bigger treadmill.

## The earn→own shift (the Ownership Line)
Every dollar you make crosses one of two paths. Run each one through a single question — call it the
**Ownership Line**: *does this dollar leave when I stop?* Rent, subscriptions, the newer car, the
dinners out — that money leaves the second the paycheck does. That's income flowing straight back
out the door. But a dollar you turn into something that pays you, or grows without you, crossed the
line. It stopped being your employee for a day and became your employee for life. That one question
separates looking okay from being free.

## What an asset actually is
Forget the jargon. An asset does one job: it puts money *in* your pocket without you trading an hour
for it. A liability does the opposite — it quietly takes money *out*. The flashy car costs you every
month, so it isn't an asset. It just looks like one. You don't need to understand markets to use
this test; you need to know which direction a thing moves your money.

## The first asset to build is the gap
Here's the part nobody tells you: the first asset you build isn't a stock, a startup, or a side gig.
It's **the gap** — the space between what you earn and what you spend. Wealth is literally that gap,
converted into things you own. Widen it, protect it fiercely, and turn it into ownership instead of
spending it. No gap, no assets — no matter how big the paycheck.

## Frequently asked questions
**Is it better to increase income or buy assets?**
Both matter, but they're different games. Raising income only builds wealth if some of it crosses
the Ownership Line into things you keep. A higher income with no gap just funds a more expensive
lifestyle. Grow the income *and* widen the gap on purpose.

**Why do high earners still end up broke?**
Because lifestyle scales with the paycheck. The raise arrives and the nicer place, car, and habits
arrive right behind it, so the gap between earning and keeping stays at zero. Without ownership,
there's nothing catching the money as it flows past.

**What counts as an asset for a normal person?**
Anything that puts money in your pocket without you trading hours for it, or grows in value while
you sleep. The simplest starting point isn't exotic — it's the gap between what you earn and spend,
protected and redirected into ownership over time.

**How do I start if I don't earn much?**
Start with the gap, not the market. Even a small, protected gap is your first asset and the raw
material for every asset after it. Run the Ownership Line on your spending, move one dollar from the
"leaves" column to the "stays" column, and repeat.
