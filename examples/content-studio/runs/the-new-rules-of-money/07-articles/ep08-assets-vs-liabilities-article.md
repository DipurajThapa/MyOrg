# What Are Assets vs Liabilities, Really? The One Test That Sorts Anything You Own

---

**An asset and a liability aren't defined by what a thing *is* — they're defined by which way the
money moves. An asset puts money *into* your pocket. A liability takes money *out*. That's the whole
rule.** Forget the accounting definition you half-remember from school, where an asset is "anything
you own that has value." That version is technically true and practically useless, because "things I
own" and "things that make me wealthier" are two completely different lists.

Here's the test. Picture an arrow between you and the thing you own, and ask which way it points each
month. If money flows *from the thing to you*, it's an asset. If money flows *from you to the thing* —
fuel, insurance, interest, upkeep, the subscription — it's a liability. No spreadsheet required. Just
the arrow. This is why people who *own* a lot can still *have* nothing: their possessions are quietly
pointing the arrow the wrong way.

Run it on the thing you're proudest of. That financed car burns fuel, needs insurance, loses value
the moment you drive off, and charges interest — four arrows, all pointing away. It's not evil; it's
just honest to call a bill a bill. **The new rule of money: an asset pays you; a liability costs you.**

## Why the schoolbook definition quietly bankrupts smart people
The definition you were handed measures the wrong thing. In school, an asset is anything you own with
value — so your car, your couch, and the shoes in your closet all count. Technically correct.
Practically, it teaches you to confuse *owning* with *wealth*. The old definition counts what you
*have*. The new one counts what your stuff *does*. That single shift is the difference between a full
garage and a growing net worth.

## The Arrow Test: sort anything in five seconds
The tool is deliberately simple, because a rule you can't remember under pressure is a rule you won't
use. Picture an arrow between you and the item. Every month, which way does it point?

- Arrow points **at you** (money in) → asset.
- Arrow points **away from you** (money out) → liability.

That's it. No app, no accounting degree, no forty-category spreadsheet. The power of the Arrow Test is
that it strips away how you *feel* about the object and looks only at the cash flow it creates. Feelings
lie. Arrows don't.

## Is your car an asset?
For most people, no. A car is one of the most expensive liabilities they'll ever own. It burns fuel,
demands insurance, loses value the second it leaves the lot, and — if financed — charges interest on
top. Four arrows, every one pointing away. That's not an attack on cars; it's clarity. When you stop
mistaking a bill for a blessing, you can finally plan around the cost instead of pretending it's an
investment.

## Is your house an asset?
The Arrow Test doesn't care about your feelings here either. A home can behave as *either* one. If it
only ever takes — mortgage interest, property taxes, repairs, the boiler that dies in January — the
arrow points away, and right now it behaves like a liability. That's not an insult to your home; it's
the truth that lets you plan honestly instead of assuming the biggest thing you own is automatically
building your wealth.

## Your one move: re-label, don't buy
Here's the exercise, and it costs nothing. Don't buy a single new thing. Just make two columns — *pays
me* and *costs me* — and drop every big item you own into the honest column. The financed car, the
"invested-in" wardrobe, the phone on a 24-month plan: liabilities. The skill that earns, the thing
others pay to use, the money set aside quietly working instead of sitting still: assets. You don't need
many. You need the arrows pointing the right way. When you can see the arrows, the reason money keeps
vanishing stops being a mystery — it's all sitting in the wrong column.

## Frequently asked questions
**Is my car an asset or a liability?**
For most people it's a liability. Unless the car directly earns you money, it takes money out every
month through fuel, insurance, depreciation, and interest. The arrow points away — so it's a liability,
no matter how much you love it.

**Is my house an asset?**
It depends on cash flow, not on the fact that you own it. If your home only ever costs you — interest,
taxes, upkeep — it currently behaves like a liability. It becomes an asset only when the arrow flips and
it puts money into your pocket rather than pulling it out.

**What actually counts as an asset then?**
Anything that reliably points the arrow back at you: a skill that earns, something you own that others
pay to use, or money set aside that quietly works instead of sitting idle. The label follows the cash
flow, not the price tag.

**Why do people who own a lot still feel broke?**
Because owning isn't wealth — owning things that *pay you* is. A garage full of arrows pointing away
looks like success and drains you every month. The Arrow Test exposes the gap between looking wealthy
and being wealthy.
