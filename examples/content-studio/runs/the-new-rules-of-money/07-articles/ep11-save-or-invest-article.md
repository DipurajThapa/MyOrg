**Save first — but only a specific amount, for one specific job. Before you invest a single dollar,
build a *buffer*: a small pool of cash whose only purpose is to absorb life's surprises. Then, and
only then, you invest the gap between what you earn and what you spend.** The old rule says put
everything in and grow fast, as if life will politely leave your money alone. It won't. Two things
are close to certain: markets dip, and life eventually sends a bill you didn't plan for. Put those
together with no cash on the side, and the timing is brutal — the emergency arrives *while* your
balance is down, and you're forced to sell at a loss to cover it.

That's the moment most beginners quit. Not because they picked wrong, but because they were in a bad
*position*. They had no shock absorber, so the first pothole threw them off the road — and they
conclude investing "doesn't work for people like them." It wasn't the investment that failed; it was
the order. The buffer isn't the opposite of investing — it's the thing that makes investing
survivable. **The new rule is quieter: buffer first, then invest the gap, patiently.**

## Why investing before you're ready makes people quit
"Invest everything, grow fast" assumes life will leave your money alone. It won't. Markets dip on
their own schedule, and life sends unplanned bills on its own schedule, and eventually those two
schedules collide. When they do — with zero cash on the side — you're forced to sell into the dip to
cover the emergency. You sell low, not because you chose to, but because you had no other option.

That's the quitting moment. Not a bad pick — a bad position. And most people who hit it don't get
back on the road. They decide investing is a rigged game and stop, when the real problem was starting
without a shock absorber. People rarely quit investing because they chose wrong. They quit because
they were forced to sell.

## The Two-Bucket Order
Here's the order that fixes it — the **Two-Bucket Order**.

**Bucket one is your buffer.** Boring, safe, easy to reach. Its only job is to catch emergencies so
nothing else has to. A common rule of thumb is a few months of your basic expenses — enough that a
broken car is an inconvenience, not a crisis. You fill bucket one *first*.

**Bucket two is your build bucket** — the gap you invest for the long run. And here's the whole
point: bucket one is what lets you *leave bucket two alone*. When an emergency hits, it drains the
boring bucket, not the growing one. Your investments ride out the dip instead of getting sold into
it. The buffer's real job isn't to earn — it's to keep you from ever having to touch what does.

## Patience beats timing
Once both buckets exist, a new temptation shows up, and it's the one that quietly undoes everything:
the urge to *time* it. Wait for the perfect dip. Jump out before the crash. Get back in at the
bottom. It feels like control. But it's the same panic from the beginning of this story, just wearing
a suit. Trying to outguess the market is how calm money turns into scared money that buys high and
sells low, again and again.

The new rule doesn't ask you to be clever. It asks you to be consistent. Fill the buffer. Invest the
gap on a steady rhythm you can actually keep. Then do the hardest thing in all of money: nothing. You
don't need to time the market — you need time in the calm, and the patience to leave it alone.

## Your one move this week
Name the bucket you're actually in.

- **No buffer yet?** Everything goes to bucket one until it's full. Building the buffer *is* the smart
  money move, even though it feels boring and slow.
- **Buffer done?** Then invest the gap on a schedule you can keep — and leave it alone.

That's it. The order does the heavy lifting.

## Frequently asked questions
**Should I save or invest first?**
Save first — but only a buffer for emergencies, not everything. Once that buffer exists, invest the
gap between what you earn and what you spend. It's not either/or; it's an order.

**How big should the buffer be before I start investing?**
Conceptually, enough that a normal life surprise is an inconvenience rather than a crisis — a common
rule of thumb is a few months of basic expenses. The exact number is personal; the principle is that
it's large enough to keep you from ever selling investments to cover an emergency.

**Why do so many beginners quit investing?**
Usually because they invested with no buffer, hit an emergency while the market was down, and were
forced to sell at a loss. They blame the investment, but the real issue was the order they did things
in.

**Isn't it smarter to wait for a dip to invest?**
Timing the market feels like control but usually behaves like panic — it leads to buying high and
selling low. Consistency tends to beat cleverness here: invest on a steady rhythm and leave it alone.
